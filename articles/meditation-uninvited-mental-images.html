Why Meditation Shows You Faces You Never Summoned — The Science of Involuntary Imagery

September 14, 2026 · 7 min read

She wrote it down the moment the practice ended, because she was afraid the feeling would evaporate before she could describe it. The instruction had been ordinary: bring to mind the person closest to you. What arrived was her mother's face — not a vague sense of her, but the face, every expression legible, "as if she were sitting right beside me." Then a smaller, entirely uninvited image: the family cat she had not touched in months, and a prick of guilt she had not been looking for.

This comes from the practice logs of a Chinese-language meditation community, where members record what happened after each session. It is one person's account, not a study, and it tells us nothing about whether a mother somewhere else felt anything. But the psychology behind what she describes is well documented, and it is stranger and more useful than the mystical reading usually pasted onto it.

Imagery is not the soft version of seeing

Start with what she was actually doing. In an fMRI study at Harvard, Ganis, Thompson and Kosslyn (2004) had people either look at faint line drawings or visualize them, then answer questions they could only answer if they were genuinely using the image. Their conclusion: visual imagery and visual perception "draw on most of the same neural machinery." Not a metaphor — an overlap in activated tissue.

The honest caveat matters, though. The overlap was not complete, and it was far stronger in frontal and parietal regions, which do control, than in the temporal and occipital regions that process real sensory input. So imagery is a genuine visual event with its own signature. It is not a dim photocopy of perception, and it is not nothing.

Why the face arrives without being called

Two ordinary mechanisms explain the uninvited part. First, the instruction was a cue. "The person closest to you" is a retrieval prompt, and the mind answers prompts with whatever is most accessible. Accessible is not the same as chosen. Second, emotional weight functions as a priority flag: heavily charged material is retrieved sooner and in more detail than neutral material. Her mother came first because that bond is load-bearing, not because anything was communicating.

Why the image felt physical is also studied. In a review in Clinical Psychology Review, Holmes and Mathews (2010) found that imagery evokes greater emotional response than verbal description does, and that because imagery and perception share processing, we can respond "as if" to an event that is only being imagined. That is the mechanism behind "as if she were sitting right beside me." It is a known property of the system, and it is exactly why imagery is used in clinical treatment rather than filed under decoration.

Why the same instruction does nothing for some people

If you have done guided practice and seen nothing — no face, no light, just words — you are not doing it wrong. Vividness of visual imagery varies enormously between people, and it is measurable; the standard instrument, the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire, has been in use since Marks (1973).

At the low end sits aphantasia, named by Zeman, Dewar and Della Sala (2015) in Cortex, after 21 people contacted them describing a lifelong absence of mental imagery. Their vividness scores were far below those of controls. The most useful finding in that paper is a dissociation: voluntary imagery was absent or badly reduced, while involuntary imagery — dreaming — was usually intact. The faculty that guided meditation leans on is specific and unevenly distributed. It can be missing while the rest of the imagination works fine. Zeman's group also cited earlier estimates that roughly 2–3% of people report no visual imagination at all.

What this is not

A vivid image of a person is not evidence of contact with that person. No study shows that imagery transmits or receives information about anyone else, and there is no proposed mechanism for it. What the research does show is that imagery reliably changes the person doing the imagining — which is why imagery rescripting is used to revise intrusive memories in clinical work.

It is also worth saying plainly that unusual experiences in meditation are neither proof of progress nor proof of a problem. Lindahl and colleagues (2017), in a mixed-methods study of Western Buddhist practitioners, catalogued 59 meditation-related experiences across seven domains — perceptual, affective and somatic among them — and found the same phenomenon judged very positively by some practitioners and very negatively by others. The experience is not the verdict. What you do with it is where all the variation lives.

What to do with an image you did not summon

Do not grade it. Treating a vivid image as a score — a sign you are finally "getting somewhere" — turns the next session into a performance review, and the mind quiets down about as well as a person being appraised.

Do not litigate it either. Spending the session arguing with yourself about whether the image was real wastes the only currency you have, which is attention. Note it, return to the breath.

Watch the residue, not the image. The useful signal is what remains ten minutes after the bell: calm, heaviness, grief, relief. In this practitioner's log, the striking part was not the vision of her mother but the guilt that followed the cat — an emotion the technique surfaced without being asked to. If a practice reliably ends in grief rather than settling, that is information about the material, and it deserves pacing, not more effort.

Bottom line

An image that arrives uninvited is visual imagery doing what visual imagery does: pulling up emotionally weighted material and simulating it close enough to perception that the body responds. It is a real event in your nervous system, it is measurable, and it is not a message from outside. That cuts both ways — it deflates the supernatural reading, and it makes the experience more impressive, not less.
