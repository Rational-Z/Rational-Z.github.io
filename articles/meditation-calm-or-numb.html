Calm or Numb? The Science of Whether Meditation Makes You Care Less

September 28, 2026 · 7 min read

No walk-ins that afternoon. No calls, no orders, nothing on the books. So she pulled the door shut, killed the lights, and sat down to practice in the middle of her own empty shop.

When she came out of it, the day's numbers were still bad. She was not upset about them. Not suppressing it, not talking herself into anything — just not upset. Then came the second thought, the one almost every long-term practitioner eventually has: Maybe this is what people mean by lying flat. Maybe I've just stopped caring.

The question deserves taking seriously: it has two answers indistinguishable from the inside. One is equanimity. The other is indifference. They feel identical. They are opposites.

What equanimity actually changes

Eberth, Sedlmeier and Schäfer interviewed meditators of varying experience, built a model from the transcripts, and tested it against a larger sample. Their conclusion — the PROMISE model, in Frontiers in Psychology — is that practice has two core effects: insight, a change in how you see things; and equanimity, a reduction in emotional reactivity.

Their definition of equanimity is narrower than the marketing suggests. It is not a reduction in what you perceive. It is a reduction in how often, how long and how hard you react to it:

"The lower frequency, duration, and intensity of the experienced emotions should not be confused with a less frequent or shallower experience of emotion-evoking stimuli. What changes is only the extent to which they are conceived as pleasurable or unpleasant."

The same paper pre-empts your worry. Traditions have a name for a state where emotional activity stops: indifference — the "near enemy" of equanimity. Near enemy means it looks the same from outside. It is not the same thing. An absence of feeling is not the goal; a quieter reaction to feeling is.

The uncomfortable evidence

Brown, Goodman and Inzlicht recorded EEG from 46 people viewing emotional images, measuring the late positive potential — a brainwave signature of how motivationally relevant something is to you. People higher in dispositional mindfulness showed a smaller response to high-arousal unpleasant images, even after controlling for attentional control.

The authors' framing is blunt. Mindfulness, they wrote, "may temper the early response to unpleasant and other motivationally salient affective stimuli before a subsequent emotional response has the opportunity to arise."

That is the strongest version of the numb worry: not that you react less, but that the signal arrives quieter. Two caveats — the study is cross-sectional, comparing people who are already more mindful rather than testing what training does, and one brainwave measure is not a life. Still, "have I gone numb?" is not needless anxiety; a solid result keeps the question open.

Meditation subtracts. It doesn't add.

Goyal and colleagues at Johns Hopkins pooled 47 randomized trials covering 3,515 participants, comparing meditation programs against active controls. Their results, in JAMA Internal Medicine: moderate evidence of improvement in anxiety, depression and pain; low evidence of no effect, or none, on positive mood, attention, sleep and weight; and no evidence meditation beat drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies.

The asymmetry explains the empty-shop feeling. Meditation is largely a subtractive technology. It takes noise out. It does not install light. If your practice feels like less — less agitation, less dread, less replaying — you are getting the effect the trials actually measured.

If it feels flat, you trained half the instrument

If the calm curdles into flatness, there is a fix. Fredrickson and colleagues found that eight weeks of loving-kindness practice increased positive emotions, which accumulated into durable personal resources over time. Watching the breath quiets the alarm; kindness practice builds the warmth. If your practice has been one hundred percent breath-watching and your life feels one hundred percent quieter, you have been running half the machine.

Creswell and Lindsay put it crisply in Current Directions in Psychological Science: mindfulness buffers stress not by numbing you, but by making you notice what is happening and accept it. Numbness cannot notice. Equanimity notices everything and refuses to be dragged.

A three-question test

You cannot settle this by introspection: both states feel the same from inside. You settle it by function:

  • Did you perceive accurately? She knew the till was empty. She did not tell herself the day had gone well. Indifference blurs the data. Equanimity reports it cleanly.
  • Did you still act? She was back the next day. Indifference cancels the plan. Equanimity only removes the panic from it.
  • Do good things still land? If nothing lands — if you cannot enjoy anything either — the question stopped being contemplative and became clinical. Loss of interest and loss of pleasure arriving together is a described symptom, not a stage of development. That is a conversation with a doctor, not more cushion time.

Her account also mentions a column of white light, trembling, a shock-like jolt. Such experiences are reported widely and are not a scoreboard. Intense sensation during stillness is attention doing something unfamiliar, not evidence of rank. The next morning's behavior is the only receipt that clears.

What the empty shop actually showed

Her calm was not the absence of caring. She checked the numbers. She thought about tomorrow. What was missing was the suffering about the numbers — the change the PROMISE authors describe. The fact is unchanged; the alarm attached to the fact is quieter.

If you are worried you have gone numb, do not consult the feeling. Consult the behavior. Still seeing clearly, still moving, still capable of being moved by something good — that is not indifference wearing a meditation costume. That is the thing you were training for.

The short version

Equanimity cuts the frequency, duration and intensity of your reactions — not your perception of events (Eberth, Sedlmeier & Schäfer, 2019). Indifference is its "near enemy": identical surface, opposite mechanism in reverse. Meditation's best-evidenced effects are on anxiety, depression and pain, not on making you happy (Goyal et al., 2014). It buffers stress by increasing notice-and-accept, not by numbing (Creswell & Lindsay, 2014). The test is functional, not felt.
